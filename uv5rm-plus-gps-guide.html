Before you press PTT: licence and legal

Listening is fine anywhere in the radio's range. Transmitting on its ham bands (144–148 MHz and 420–450 MHz on the US version) requires an amateur radio licence in the US (Technician class or higher) and a similar licence in most countries. The radio's own manual states it is not legal for FRS, GMRS, MURS or PMR446 channels, even though it can be tuned there. Weather (NOAA), airband and FM broadcast are receive-only.

Your GPS version is not the same as the standard UV-5RM Plus

Most videos and guides online cover the non-GPS model. The GPS version runs different firmware, so follow this guide (built from Baofeng's GPS-version manual, print V1.0) when they disagree.

Out of the box

What's in the box

Radio body, lithium-ion battery pack, antenna, belt clip, wrist strap, charger and manual. Bundles vary: some add an earpiece, a longer antenna, a speaker-mic or a programming cable.

1 · Antenna

Hold the antenna at its base and screw it clockwise onto the top connector until snug. Never transmit without an antenna attached; it can damage the transmitter.

2 · Battery

With the radio off, lay the battery on the back about 1–2 cm below the top edge, lined up with the rails, and slide it up until it clicks. To remove, press the release latch above the battery and slide it down.

3 · Belt clip and earpiece

The clip fixes with the two screws above the battery. The earpiece or speaker-mic plugs into the two-pin (Kenwood-style) jack under the rubber flap on the side; push it fully home or the radio may think PTT is pressed.

4 · Charging

  • USB-C: the port is on the battery itself. Turn the radio off, plug in, and the indicator shows charging. An empty battery takes about 4 hours.
  • Desk charger (if included): light blinks orange when ready, red while charging, green when full. Takes roughly 2–5 hours. If the radio is switched on in the cradle the light may never turn green.
  • Charge fully before first use. A new battery reaches full capacity after two or three full charge cycles.
  • Charge between 5 °C and 40 °C. Don't charge a wet, swollen or damaged battery, and don't leave it on the charger for days.
  • For storage, leave the battery partly charged and somewhere cool and dry.

The two modes you must know

Frequency (VFO) mode is a tuning dial: type any frequency and go. Good for experimenting and for setting up a channel before saving it.

Channel (MR) mode is a list of saved memories with all their settings. This is what you use day to day. Channels live inside zones: up to 10 zones of 64 channels each.

Switch with VFO/MR. Hold VFO/MR to change how channels are displayed: number, frequency or name.

    On-air basics

    Making a call (radio to radio)

    1. Listen first for 10–20 seconds. If the frequency is busy, wait.
    2. Hold PTT, pause half a second, then: “W1ABC, this is K2XYZ.” Their call sign first, yours second.
    3. Release and listen. No answer after two or three tries: “K2XYZ clear.”
    4. To call anyone at all: “K2XYZ, monitoring” or “CQ, CQ, this is K2XYZ.”
    5. Keep each transmission short and leave a second of silence before replying so others can break in.

    Using a repeater

    • Listen for a while before your first transmission to learn how that repeater's users talk.
    • To announce yourself: “K2XYZ, listening.” Don't call CQ on a repeater.
    • After the other station stops, wait for the courtesy beep (or a second) before keying. That resets the repeater's timer and leaves room for emergencies.
    • Most repeaters cut you off after about 3 minutes of continuous talking.
    • To join a conversation, say your call sign between transmissions. Say “break” only for something urgent.
    • If you can hear each other directly, move to a simplex frequency and free the repeater.

    Identifying (US rules)

    Give your call sign at least every 10 minutes during a contact and at the end of it. English letters and numbers, or the phonetic alphabet. You don't need to say the other person's call sign each time, and you don't need to identify when only listening. No music, no business use, no codes meant to hide meaning, and no transmitting without identifying.

    If you need help

    On a repeater or 146.520: “Break, break, emergency, this is K2XYZ.” Then give where you are (read the coordinates from GPS mode), what happened, and what you need. Stay on that frequency. If a phone works, call 911 first. US rules allow any station to use whatever radio means it has to call for help when life or property is in immediate danger and normal communication isn't available; that is the one situation where transmitting without a licence is permitted.

    Getting licensed

    • Technician licence (US): one 35-question multiple-choice exam, 26 correct to pass, no Morse code. Every question comes from a published pool, so practice tests show you the real questions.
    • Cost: a small exam-session fee (about $15 with most groups, some free) plus the FCC's $35 application fee. The licence lasts 10 years.
    • How: study with a free app or site (HamStudy is the common one), then sit the exam in person with a local club or online by video. Most people need 10–20 hours of study.
    • Each person who transmits needs their own licence. A licensed operator can let someone else talk while they stay in control at the radio.
    • GMRS is the no-exam alternative ($35, covers your family, 10 years), but it requires a GMRS-certified radio, which this one is not.

    Set up your set of radios

    Make them all talk to each other

    Two radios hear each other only when these match on both:

    • Frequency (and offset, if using a repeater).
    • Tone: if one radio has RX CTCSS/DCS set, the other must transmit that same tone. Simplest: leave tones OFF on all of them, or set the same TX and RX tone on all.
    • Bandwidth: Wide on all, or Narrow on all.

    Save the shared frequency to the same channel number in the same zone on every radio and give it the same name, so “go to channel 2” means the same thing for everyone.

    For GPS sharing, each radio needs its own identity

    Position sharing rides on DTMF signalling. Each radio needs a unique Radio ID (Radio Info › My Radio › Radio ID, or in the PC software) and the others listed as contacts/members. All radios must be on the same channel, with that channel on the main (highlighted) line, GNSS on, and Share Position / Request Position switched on. See the GPS sharing walkthrough.

    Fastest way: set up one, copy to the rest

    Program one radio fully, read it into the programming software (see Programming on a Mac), save the file, then write that file to each of the other radios. Afterwards change each radio's ID and name so they are unique. Without a cable, the one-touch search copies one frequency and tone at a time over the air.

    Filling it in

    • Zone / Ch: where the channel lives on the radio (zones 1–10, channels 1–64).
    • Name: six characters or fewer.
    • Shift / Offset: leave blank for simplex. For a repeater pick + or − and the offset (0.600 on 2 m, 5.000 on 70 cm).
    • Tone: the CTCSS tone you transmit. Leave OFF for your own simplex channels unless you all set the same one.
    • Rows outside 144–148 / 420–450 MHz are marked listen; the export sets them receive-only.
    • Export CSV uses CHIRP's column layout. Row numbers follow the zones: Zone 1 is rows 1–64, Zone 2 is 65–128, and so on. You can also open the CSV in Numbers.
    • Save to file (top of the page) writes the plan, test log, labels and ticked boxes into the guide file itself, so they travel with it. Save plan file makes a separate backup of just the plan.

    Range & accessories

    What range to expect

    VHF and UHF travel in nearly straight lines. Height and a clear path matter far more than watts. The “10 W long range” on the box changes little: going from 5 W to 10 W adds a barely noticeable amount of signal.

    Situation (handheld to handheld)Typical
    Inside buildings, dense citya few blocks to 1 mile (0.3–1.5 km)
    Suburbs, woods, rolling ground1–3 miles (1.5–5 km)
    Open flat country or over water3–6 miles (5–10 km)
    Hilltop to valley, clear view10–30 miles (15–50 km)
    Through a repeater on a tower or mountain20–60 miles (30–100 km)
    From inside a car, stock antennaroughly half of the above

    These are rough working figures; your terrain decides. VHF (2 m) carries a little further outdoors and over hills. UHF (70 cm) gets in and out of buildings and vehicles better.

    Line-of-sight calculator

    How far two antennas can “see” each other over flat ground or water.

     

    Two people standing on flat ground get about 7 miles at best. Put one of them on a 500 ft hill and it becomes about 35. Hills, buildings and trees in between reduce it.

    More range for free

    • Get higher: an upstairs window, a rise in the ground, the roof of the car.
    • Hold the radio upright with the antenna vertical and clear of your body; don't talk with it on your belt.
    • Step outside, or at least to a window facing the other station.
    • Take a few steps: at UHF, moving one metre can turn noise into a clear signal.
    • Use a repeater when direct contact fails.

    Field readiness

    Battery planning

    Baofeng quotes about 15 hours at 5% transmit, 5% receive, 90% standby. Treat that as a best case and plan on one full day of light use per charge, less with GPS on. I don't have measured figures for the GPS drain, so test yours once before relying on it.

    What drains it fastest
    • Transmitting on high power (by far the biggest)
    • GNSS left on
    • Screen always lit, high brightness
    • Scanning and dual watch (receiver never sleeps)
    • Cold: capacity drops noticeably below freezing
    How to stretch it
    • Program Channel › Trans Power › Low for nearby contacts
    • Radio Setting › Power Save › 1:2
    • BackLight 5 s, Brightness 2
    • GNSS off until you need a position
    • Agree check-in times and switch off between them
    • Carry it inside your jacket in the cold

    Reading the voltage (put Voltage on a side key, or set Power on Display to VOLTAGE). For a 7.4 V lithium-ion pack: about 8.4 V is full, 7.4 V is roughly half, and below about 6.8 V it is nearly empty. Readings sag while transmitting.

    Pre-trip checklist

    Agree a simple plan before you split up

    • Primary channel and a backup channel on the other band (one on 2 m, one on 70 cm).
    • Check-in times: for example the first five minutes of every hour. Radios can stay off in between.
    • If you miss two check-ins: what happens next (go to a named spot, head back to the vehicle).
    • A name for each radio so calls are clear: “Radio 2, this is Radio 1.” Licensed operators add their call signs.
    • Write it on the pocket cheat sheet so everyone carries the same plan.

    When you can't reach each other

    1. Check your own radio first: volume up, right zone and channel, the correct line highlighted (A/B), keypad not locked into the wrong channel, battery not flat.
    2. Hold SK2 (monitor) while they should be transmitting. If you hear them through the hiss, it's a tone or squelch mismatch, not range.
    3. Move: a few steps first, then to higher or more open ground. Hold the radio high and upright.
    4. Switch to high power for the call.
    5. At the next agreed time, try the backup channel.
    6. Try a repeater you both have programmed.
    7. If you had GPS sharing working earlier, your radio still shows their last received position in the member list.
    8. Follow the missed-check-in plan. Don't wander out of the area you agreed on.

    Emergency family channels

    Choose them by listening and testing from the places your family will actually be. A frequency that looks good on a list can be busy or unreachable where you live.

    First decide who will be transmitting

    On these radios, everyone who transmits needs a ham licence, including for practice drills. The only exception is a genuine emergency where life or property is in immediate danger and no other means is available. If some family members won't get licensed, give them FRS radios: your Baofengs can listen to FRS channels but can't legally talk on them. A plan you aren't allowed to practise is a plan that fails, so sort this out first. See getting licensed.

    The method

    1. Start from the simplex parts of the band, using your region's band plan. Nationally, 2 m FM simplex sits around 146.40–146.58 and 147.42–147.57 MHz, and 70 cm simplex around 445–447 MHz, but regional coordinators change the details. Don't make the calling frequencies (146.520, 446.000) your home channel, since everyone uses those.
    2. Load candidates and listen for a week. Use the button below, write them to the radio, and scan them at different times of day. Cross off any with regular traffic.
    3. Keep three channels:
      • Primary on 2 m: carries further outdoors and over hills.
      • Backup on 70 cm: better in and out of buildings and cars.
      • A local repeater, ideally one with backup power that the local emergency group uses, for when you are too far apart for direct contact.
    4. Test from the real locations: home, work, school, the usual routes. Mark where it fails; those spots need the repeater or an agreed meeting point.
    5. Set a transmit tone on every radio and leave receive tone off. You hear everything on the channel, and you can switch receive tone on later if chatter becomes a problem. A tone is not privacy: anyone can listen.
    6. Same slots on every radio: Zone 1, channels 1–3, same names, plus your strongest NOAA channel.
    7. Agree check-in windows, such as the first five minutes of every hour, so radios stay off in between and batteries last days.
    8. Practise monthly. Five minutes, everyone on the air from a different place.

    Before you rely on one

    • Rows this radio can't use (other bands, digital-only modes) are shown greyed out and can't be ticked. A repeater that needs a DCS code instead of a tone is imported without one; set it by hand.
    • Test each: key up for a second, say your call sign, release. A beep or a short burst of carrier means you got in.
    • For an emergency plan, find out which repeaters have backup power and an emergency role: ask on the air or check the owning club's website.
    • Repeaters are run by volunteers and clubs. In a regional disaster they may be reserved for emergency traffic, overloaded or off the air, which is why your simplex channels stay the primary plan.
    • Listings change. Repeat the search once or twice a year.

    Ordering your list, and why

    Once you have simplex candidates and local repeaters, arrange Zone 1 so the most important channels are the easiest to reach:

    SlotsWhatWhy

    How to use it

    Programming on a Mac

    Status checked November 2026. Software support for the GPS version is still moving, so this section says plainly what works, what is experimental and what needs Windows.

    1 · The cable

    • You need a K-plug programming cable (two-pin Kenwood-style plug into the earpiece jack). The USB-C port on the battery only charges; it carries no data.
    • Buy a cable with a CH340 or FTDI chip. macOS has had drivers for both built in since 10.14 Mojave. Do not install any driver for them; old third-party CH340 drivers cause trouble on current macOS.
    • Avoid “PL2303” cables. Most cheap ones use counterfeit Prolific chips that the official Mac driver deliberately refuses. If yours is one, replacing the cable is quicker than fighting it.
    • On a Mac with only USB-C ports, use a plain USB-A to USB-C adapter or a hub that passes data.

    2 · Check the Mac sees it

    Plug the cable into the Mac (radio not needed yet), open Terminal and run:

    ls /dev/cu.*

    A working cable adds an entry such as /dev/cu.usbserial-1420 or /dev/cu.wchusbserial1420. Unplug and run it again: the entry should disappear. If nothing new appears, the cable (or adapter) is the problem, not the software. Always choose the cu. port in programming software, not tty.

    Route B · Baofeng's CPS in a Windows virtual machine

    1. Install a virtual machine app (UTM is free; Parallels and VMware Fusion also work) and set up Windows 11. On Apple Silicon Macs this is Windows 11 for ARM, which runs ordinary Windows programs through its built-in emulation.
    2. Download the UV-5RM Plus GPS CPS from baofengradio.com/pages/download inside the VM. Get the GPS one; the standard UV-5RM Plus CPS is a different program.
    3. Plug in the cable and pass the USB device through to the VM (in UTM: the USB icon in the window toolbar; in Parallels: Devices › USB). It then disappears from macOS and shows up in Windows as a COM port.
    4. Windows needs its own driver for the cable chip. On Apple Silicon it must be an ARM64 driver; WCH (CH340) and FTDI both publish one. Check Device Manager › Ports for the COM number.
    5. In the CPS: choose that COM port, Read from the radio, save the file as your backup, edit, then Write.

    A side benefit of the VM: CHIRP community members report that some copies of Baofeng's CPS and firmware updater are flagged by virus scanners. Keeping them inside a VM that has no access to your Mac's files is the cautious way to run them.

    Route C · CHIRP on macOS

    Where it stands: CHIRP's built-in “Baofeng UV-5RM Plus” entry is for the older, non-GPS firmware and does not match your radio. Support for the GPS / zone firmware (the same platform as the BF-5RH Pro) is being developed in CHIRP issue #11669 and GitHub pull request #1625. As of November 2026 neither is in an official build, and the CHIRP maintainer reported uploads failing on his own unit.

    If you want to try it anyway:

    1. Install CHIRP-next for macOS from chirpmyradio.com (pick the Apple Silicon or Intel build). First launch: right-click the app › Open, or allow it under System Settings › Privacy & Security.
    2. Check your firmware on the radio: MENU › Radio Info › Versions. The experimental driver was tested on software v2_0_09 / hardware v1_0_4.
    3. Download the latest driver module (.py file) attached to CHIRP issue #11669.
    4. In CHIRP: Help › tick Developer Mode, restart CHIRP, then File › Load Module and choose the .py file. It has to be reloaded each time CHIRP starts.
    5. Radio › Download from radio. Port: your /dev/cu.usbserial…. Vendor Baofeng, model BF-5RH-Pro (the name the module registers).
    6. Save that first download untouched as a backup before changing anything.
    7. Edit and upload. Rows 1–64 are Zone 1, 65–128 Zone 2, and so on.
    Known problems with the experimental driver: uploads that fail or loop on some radios; one report of zones being wiped after a bad write (fixed by restoring the backup); the per-channel speaker-mute option isn't represented; DTMF, contacts and GPS settings are unverified or absent. Keep the Windows CPS route available as your recovery path.

    Programming tips that save grief

    • Always read from the radio first and build on that file. Baofeng warns that writing a file that didn't start as a read can cause errors.
    • One backup per radio, named with its ID and the date, kept somewhere safe. It is your undo button.
    • Seat the plug hard; a half-seated K-plug is the most common cause of “radio did not respond”. Radio on, fully charged, on a quiet channel, nothing transmitting nearby.
    • Don't touch the radio or cable during a write. If a write fails, power-cycle the radio and write again before panicking.
    • Plan zones on paper first: for example Zone 1 your group's simplex channels, Zone 2 local repeaters, Zone 3 listen-only (marine, rail). 64 channels each.
    • Put your most-used channel at position 1 of Zone 1; that is what the radio lands on after a reset.
    • Cloning a set: finish one radio, read it, write that same file to the others, then change each one's Radio ID and name so GPS sharing can tell them apart.
    • Keep names to six characters or fewer so they display in full.
    • In CHIRP you can export to CSV and edit the channel list in Numbers, then import it back.
    • Leave firmware alone unless something is actually broken. Reports describe newer firmware built for a different circuit board revision causing keypad and backlight faults.

    Things only the PC software can set

    2-Tone and 5-Tone sequences, the contact list, the power-on picture (a 160×128 .bmp), and the full button assignments. The power-on password can also be read and cleared there.

    Unlock guides

    Unlock the keypad

    Hold ✱ (padlock key) for about two seconds. If AutoLock is on, press MENU then ✱. To stop it re-locking: MENU › Radio Setting › AutoLock › OFF. PTT and the side keys work even when locked.

    Remove or recover the power-on password

    You know it: MENU › Radio Setting › Password, delete every digit with EXIT, then MENU to save.

    You forgot it: connect the programming cable, read the radio with the CPS and clear the password field there, then write it back. Programming mode works without entering the password on the radio.

    Get out of Chinese menus

    MENU, 3 (third entry, Radio Setting), MENU, 8, MENU, choose English, MENU. Then the same with 7 for the voice.

    Transmit frequency range (“band unlock”)

    The US version receives 108–520 MHz in pieces but only transmits on 144–148 and 420–450 MHz. That limit is set in firmware.

    What I could and couldn't confirm for the GPS version:

    • No confirmed unlock exists for the UV-5RM Plus GPS. In the main community thread tracking this radio family, nobody has reported a successful GPS-model unlock, and the old UV-5R trick (holding keys at power-on until “Factory” appears) is not reported to work on it.
    • Check your radio's region: on related 5RM/5RH radios, holding 8 while switching on shows the lock state: “FCC” means US-limited, “BF” means open. Unverified on the GPS model, harmless to try.
    • Vendor “special” CPS: Baofeng and Radioddity have handed out modified CPS builds with a frequency-limit editor (one is named 5RM_CPS_special_ver_v1.2.0) to customers who ask support. Users report it working on non-GPS siblings and failing to connect on others. One user described the change as a one-way trip.
    • Firmware swaps that open up sibling models are reported as irreversible, because the original firmware isn't available to go back to, and wrong-board firmware has caused keypad and backlight faults.
    Before going further: an unlock doesn't change what you may legally transmit on. A ham licence covers the ham bands the radio already transmits on; GMRS, FRS, MURS, marine and business channels each require a radio certified for that service, which this one isn't. Airband transmit is not possible on this hardware at all. Given the brick risk and nothing gained legally, my recommendation is to leave the transmit limits alone and, if you need GMRS, buy a GMRS-certified handheld.

    Getting more to listen to (no unlock needed)

    Reception is already open across 108–136, 136–174, 220–260, 350–390 and 400–520 MHz. Type the frequency in VFO mode, or set a sweep with MENU › Scan › Freq Ranger. Saved listen-only channels work too: just don't press PTT on them, or set TX Admit so the radio blocks it on a busy channel.
